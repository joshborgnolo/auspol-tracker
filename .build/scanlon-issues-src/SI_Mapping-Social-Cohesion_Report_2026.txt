                                                      Social cohesion in 2026




It is important to remember that recent trends
are reflecting, if not driven by, major global
upheaval. The terrorist attack at Bondi Beach
was a uniquely horrific global event, though
the world has also struggled with antisemitism,
Islamophobia, racism and deepening social and
political divisions over issues like immigration.27
The fact that three quarters of Australians still
believe that multiculturalism has been good for
the country even after three years of decline
is perhaps testament to the symbolic value of
multiculturalism in Australia and its potential
– alongside community bonds – to hold social
cohesion together during difficult times.

Symbolic support for multiculturalism
evidently though does not protect Australia
against racism, discrimination and animosity
towards people from different ethnic and
cultural backgrounds. This shows that
acknowledgement of multiculturalism is not
enough and that the current threats to social
cohesion are serious and in need of a concerted
public and community response.

Social cohesion in Australia is unlikely to be
strengthened or maintained through consensus
or conformity. Australians are diverse socially,
politically and culturally and each of us
deserve the right to fight for what we believe
in, to speak out for all that is important to us
and against injustice, violence and harm in
our communities and the world. By the same
token, we must recognise that our words and
actions can affect the happiness, safety and
belonging of others – all of the things that
survey respondents tell us are most important
for social cohesion. A socially cohesive society
is therefore not one where we think and look
alike, but where we argue, debate, protest and
fight against injustice, all while remembering
the common humanity that binds us all.

Social cohesion in
a challenging and
complex world
Social cohesion means many different things
to many different people. As an idea and
concept, social cohesion has a long history
and, like today, has long been used to advocate
for personal and sectional interests as well as
public good. In a diverse society like modern
Australia, where there is no prospect of us all
thinking and looking alike and where there are
important and widely felt social, political and
economic pressures and challenges, it is useful
to consider what people think are important
to social cohesion. As we have explained
in this chapter, there is a clear message on
this. Amidst division and disgruntlement,
conflict and financial stress, people most
value the degree to which society provides
for the happiness, safety and belonging of all
of us, where people’s active involvement in
community is welcome but on their own terms
and where differences are fine but mutual
respect through our differences is vital.

Looking beyond the
headline numbers
The overall Scanlon Index of social cohesion
is currently at its lowest level in the 20-year
history of the Mapping Social Cohesion study.
The long run decline has been driven by a
weakening sense of national pride, belonging
and social justice, coupled with strains
on happiness and personal and financial
wellbeing, increasing pessimism and elevated
levels of discrimination. Other evidence
points to long-run declines in social and
community participation and, in more recent
years, sustained financial pressures, weaker
and more polarised trust in government and
institutions and support for multiculturalism
and immigrant diversity.

where each of us draws a sense of purpose, fulfillment and satisfaction
from living in communities and a society that look out for each other.

are seeing indications of declining happiness and wellbeing, particularly
for younger and middle aged adults. At the same time, cost-of-living
pressures continue to strain our financial wellbeing, which in turn looks
to be pressuring our perceived sense of social cohesion.

The pressures on happiness
and wellbeing speak to the
challenges and priorities for
a socially cohesive Australia
While related to social cohesion and broader
social challenges and pressures, personal
wellbeing for Australians, as for others, is
generally resilient and not easily shifted.
As we have shown in this chapter, changes
in happiness and wellbeing are generally
slow moving and influenced by personal and
life stage changes more than society-wide
change. There does though appear to be a
long-run trend towards a weaker sense of
wellbeing in Australia, particularly among
younger and middle-aged adults. This shift
is also reflected in several other sources of
information including the Australian Bureau
of Statistics General Social Survey, the World
Happiness Report and the ANUPoll series.
There do not appear to be easy explanations or
solutions, though the collective evidence points
to personal and societal changes that have
diminished social connections and belonging
and have combined with economic changes
to increase financial and other pressures
and uncertainties of modern life. The results
have not been catastrophic to date, though
as people attest, addressing these pressures
and providing a stronger foundation for the
happiness and good life of all are among the
most important priorities for a cohesive society.

Major national and global events, trends and forces can impact on social
cohesion in Australia in various ways. Conflict, violence and political
division fill the world and our news and social media feeds, if not also our
personal lives, shaping our sense of security, social justice, hope for our
collective future and views on government and the wider world. At the
heart of socially cohesive societies, however, are cohesive communities
and the daily interactions people have with friends, family, neighbours,
colleagues, acquaintances and strangers. So while the wider world has
its impacts, many of the things that are foundational to social cohesion
– including a sense of belonging, acceptance and trust in others, as well
as active participation and social engagement – are formed and maintained
in daily life and in communities and not easily shifted in the face of the
troubles of the wider world.

Community connections are
resilient and vital to national
social cohesion – but in need
of continual support
Communities are at the coalface of social
cohesion in providing the environment for daily
social interaction, connection and participation
and providing a basis for belonging,
acceptance and trust in people. In this way,
communities potentially help to guard against
the effects of crises and conflict on wellbeing
at a personal level and social cohesion at a
national level. While it is difficult to prove
conclusively, this continues to be the case – at
least on the available evidence – in Australia
today. Neighbourhood connection and cohesion
continue to be resilient in 2026, one of the few
unquestionably positive points to emerge from
this year’s study. And although increasingly
isolated among mostly bad news, the strength
of neighbourhood connection this year remains
related to wellbeing, belonging, trust and
acceptance and, if nothing else, may be helping
to prevent deeper decline and division in these
areas. That said though, general trust in people
is under pressure if not weakening, while
active participation in social and community
life appears less common than it was 16
years ago. These trends sit alongside others
recorded in the Mapping Social Cohesion
study and other sources, including declining
happiness and social connection, suggesting
that communities continue to be relevant and
important – though also vulnerable and not
to be taken for granted or treated as the only
resource to underpin national social cohesion.

Despite discrimination and a weaker sense of
belonging, overseas-born Australians overall
have a stronger sense of social cohesion
across most domains. Across 2024-2026,
overseas-born Australians have been at least
as likely as the Australian-born to take pride
in the Australian way of life and culture, to
participate in their communities and to get on
well with their neighbours and more likely to
believe in the fair go, to trust the Government
and political system and to support diversity
and Australian multiculturalism.

Estimates of social cohesion indicators
in 2024-2026 are shown in Figure 41 by
respondents’ place of birth. Averaging over
2024, 2025 and 2026, 79% of those born in
south and central Asia and 64-67% of those
born in East Asia and the Pacific, Africa and
the Middle East and Europe, the Americas and
New Zealand agree that ‘Australia is a land
of economic opportunity where in the long
run, hard work brings a better life’, compared
with 57% of those born in Australia. An
average of almost one-half (47%) of those
born in south and central Asia and 34-38%
of other overseas-born Australians trust the
Federal Government all or most of the time
compared with 31% of the Australian-born.
Perhaps unsurprisingly, 74-82% of those born
across Asia, Africa and the Pacific agree that
‘accepting immigrants from many different
countries makes Australia stronger’, all higher
than for the Australian-born population (64%).
At least four-in-five (81-83%) meanwhile
agree that their neighbours from different
backgrounds get on well together, in line
with if not slightly above the figure for the
Australian born (79%). Overall then and despite
the challenges faced in establishing a sense
of place and belonging in Australia, people
who have immigrated to the country provide a
valuable source of connection and cohesion.

                                                                                          Conclusion




Mapping Social Cohesion 2026 is pointing to a society under strain. People
are more divided, less trusting, more pessimistic and less supportive of
Australia’s multicultural fabric. In a troubled world and after a difficult few
years, this is perhaps not surprising, but a cause for concern and action
nonetheless. In seeking to reinvigorate and strengthen social cohesion,
Australia may draw on several strengths particularly community cohesion
and the inclusive nature of Australian identity – alongside the insight of
many that social cohesion does not come from sameness, but from our
respect, kindness, empathy and common humanity, even as we fight for
what we believe in.
