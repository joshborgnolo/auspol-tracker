Preference flows explained

Where the site’s preference-flow estimates come from

Last election’s preference flows ≈ next election’s preference flows

It’s difficult to predict how voters will direct their preferences at the next election. So difficult, in fact, that the default position of pollsters and analysts is not to try. They prefer to solve the problem ex post rather than ex ante: using the preference flows at the last election rather than attempting to forecast them for the next election. As it turns out, this method has consistently proved to be more reliable and accurate than any other. This is for the simple reason that preference flows tend not to change much from election to election, or at least their changes tend to largely cancel each other out. But it’s also because the alternative method, using respondent-allocated flows (who voters say they will preference when polled), is strangely unreliable.

As One Nation’s primary vote has ballooned this term, Labor versus the Coalition is no longer the only central two-party-preferred contest. The classic two-party contest now shares the podium with Labor versus One Nation. This complicates things, because while we have last-election flows for Labor versus the Coalition, we don’t have them for Labor versus One Nation. But we can use the preference flows for recent state and by-elections to make an educated guess.

The ALP v L/NP flows are simply the last-election flows (LEFs). We’re assuming they won’t change much in 2028. In this distinctly abnormal political environment, this is counterintuitive: Labor and Coalition primary votes have collapsed 8–9 points from already-historic lows, and One Nation’s primary vote has soared by x points. It seems silly to assume the old rules still apply. But the historical success of last-election flows – and the historical unreliability of attempts to deviate from them – means that no last-election flow figure should be displaced without strong evidence for displacing it. And we have no such evidence yet.

Preference flows are important, but I should preface that they matter much less than first preferences. Since 2013, 90% of seats in federal elections have been won by the candidate who led on primary votes. And since 1987, LEFs (assuming perfectly divined primaries) have produced a 2PP error of 0.4 points, while pollster primaries (assuming perfectly divined preference flows) have produced a 2PP error of 1.1 points, almost three times greater.1 Alas, we can’t control the accuracy of the primary votes pollsters publish – but we can control how to interpet and use them to guage public opinion, and preference flows are an important part of this process.

The track record of last-election flows is impressive, so when and why would you deviate from them?

Let’s take a look at some examples through history.

When deviation went wrong

1. 2004: Huge industry-wide variance in respondent-allocated preference flows

Last-election and respondent-allocated flows in 2004 were the difference between an exceptional year for pollsters (+0.4 error) and an average year for pollsters (+1.5 error). Roy Morgan had it particularly bad: had they used LEFs, they would have been the most accurate pollster (−0.5). Instead, they ended up being the least accurate (+3.7).2

Pollsters expected 69–74% of minor-party preferences to reach Labor, but only 61% did. After 2004, Newspoll started using LEFs.

2. YouGov in 2025

YouGov handed down these primaries in its final poll before the 2025 election:

TableYouGov final-poll primaries; bottom row bolded with the election primaries.

With these primaries, they published a 2PP of 52.2, which ultimately put them in the less accurate half of pollsters (missing the result by −3.0). LEFs, on the other hand, would have produced a 2PP result of 53.9, which would have made YouGov’s the single most accurate poll of 2025 (−1.6), by a margin of +0.7 on other implied 2PPs and +0.9 on other published 2PPs.

Unwise in retrospect, YouGov’s stated rationale for departing from LEFs was that Labor’s 2022 preferences reflected the unpopular incumbent, Scott Morrison, and with Labor now incumbent, the LEFs could not be assumed to repeat.

2. Newspoll in 2019 and 2025: Foreseeing One Nation’s preference-flow drift towards the Coalition

In 2019, One Nation preferences hardened towards the Coalition against Labor by 14.4 points compared to the previous election. In 2025, they hardened a further 10.2 points.

In 2019, Newspoll saw this coming. They said:

The preference flow from Pauline Hanson’s One Nation at recent state elections has shifted substantially since the 2016 federal election when just 50.5 per cent of their preferences went to the Coalition. At the West Australian and Queensland elections the party did not preference against the sitting member and this resulted in the following preference flow from One Nation to the Liberal Party/LNP: WA (60.6 per cent [60.7% on this site]); Queensland (65.2 per cent [66.3% on this site]).

When we noted this shift in preferences we recalibrated the Newspoll preference flow allocation from One Nation to the Coalition from 50 to 60 per cent.

Here are the preference flows they had to work with:

Newspoll’s published 2PP missed by +3.0 points in 2019, while their implied 2PP missed by +3.9. Although they still missed by a significant margin (about as much as the average pollster’s miss), their depature from LEFs reduced their 2PP error significantly.

In 2025, Newpoll did the same thing. To quote:

Campbell White, head of Pyxis Polling & Insights which independently administers Newspoll for The Australian said: “Newspoll uses previous election flows to calculate two-party-preferred because it has consistently proved to be more reliable than other methods. However, based on what happened at the Queensland state election and through questions where we ask respondents about their intentions, it is clear that One Nation preferences are likely to flow more strongly to the Coalition than they did at the 2022 federal election.

“For this reason, we are adjusting the calculation of preferences accordingly.”

Again, here are the preference flows they had to work with:

Interestingly, although they correctly antipated a further hardening of One Nation preferences to the Coalition, Newspoll’s published 2PP (−2.7) did not outperform its implied 2PP (−2.2) in 2025. In other words, they would have been better off had they simply used LEFs. This seems to be because while the share of One Nation preferences reaching the Coalition increased (with a net effect on election 2PP of x), the share of others’ and Greens preferences reaching the Coalition decreased (with a net effect on election 2PP of y). The two effects happened to cancel each other out, and Newspoll foresaw, and accounted for, only one of them.

The lesson here is that strong evidence of one preference-flow shift does not necessairly justify displacing the LEF-presumption, because the effect of the shift may well be offset the effect of other shifts.

4. LEF misses: 2013, 2019, 2022

In 2013 and 2022, LEFs on the correct primaries produced an error of −1.04 and −0.87 respectively. Together with the famous industry failure of 2019 (+0.82), these are the big, clear misses of the last 40 years.3

Why did they miss?

In 2013, preferences from the Greens and preferences from all other minor parties hardened towards Labor, by 4.2 and 5.1 points respectively. The same thing happened in 2022: Greens hardened by 3.5 points and others hardened by 3.9 points.

In 2019, as Newspoll broadly anticipated, preferences from One Nation hardened to the Coalition by 14.7 points. At the same time, preferences from all other minor parties hardened the same direction by 3.1 points.
