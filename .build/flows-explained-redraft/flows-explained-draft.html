Preference flows explained

Where the site’s preference-flow estimates come from

Last election’s preference flows ≈ next election’s preference flows

It’s difficult to predict how voters will direct their preferences at the next election. So difficult, in fact, that the default position of pollsters and analysts is simply not to try. They prefer to solve the problem ex post rather than ex ante: using the preference flows at the last election rather than attempting to forecast them for the next election.

As it turns out, this method has consistently proved more accurate and reliable than any other. This is for the simple reason that preference flows tend not to change much from election to election, or at least their changes tend largely to cancel each other out. But it’s also because the alternative method, respondent-allocated flows (who voters say they will preference when polled), is strangely unreliable.

As One Nation’s primary vote has ballooned this term, Labor versus the Coalition is no longer the only central two-party-preferred contest, for the first time since 1910. The classic two-party contest now shares its podium with Labor versus One Nation. This complicates things: we have last-election flows for Labor versus the Coalition, but not for Labor versus One Nation. We can, however, use the preference flows at recent state elections and by-elections to make an educated guess.

The Labor v Coalition flows are simply the last-election flows (LEFs). We’re assuming they won’t change much by 2028. In this distinctly abnormal political environment, that is counterintuitive: the Labor and Coalition primary votes have collapsed 8.2 and 8.7 points respectively from already historic lows, and One Nation’s has soared by 19.7 points. It seems silly to assume the old rules still apply. But the historical success of last-election flows, and the historical unreliability of attempts to deviate from them, mean that no last-election flow should be displaced without strong evidence. And we have no such evidence yet.

Preference flows are important, but I should preface that they matter much less than first preferences. Since 2013, 90% of seats at federal elections have been won by the candidate who led on primary votes. And since 1996, LEFs (assuming perfectly divined primaries) have produced a 2PP error of 0.5 points, while pollster primaries (assuming perfectly divined preference flows) have produced a 2PP error of 1.2 points, around two and a half times greater.1 Alas, we can’t control the accuracy of the primary votes pollsters publish. But we can control how we interpret them and use them to gauge public opinion, and preference flows are an important part of that.

The track record of last-election flows is impressive. So when, and why, would you deviate from them? Let’s look at some examples from history.

When deviation went wrong

Last-election flows and respondent-allocated flows were, in 2004, the difference between a better-than-average year for pollsters (+0.9 error) and an average one (+1.5). Roy Morgan had it particularly bad: had it used LEFs, it would have missed by +1.9; instead, it was the least accurate pollster (+3.7).2

The three pollsters using respondent-allocated preferences (Newspoll, ACNielsen and Roy Morgan) expected 69–74% of minor-party preferences to reach Labor, but only 61% did. Galaxy, which used last-election flows, published the best final 2PP of the four. Simon Jackman’s study of every poll in the 2004 campaign reached the same verdict. On average, the three phone pollsters (ACNielsen, Galaxy and Newspoll) got the Coalition’s primary vote almost exactly right, within 0.1 points. On 2PP they ran about 1.3 points too high for Labor, just short of statistical significance. He put the gap down to how each house turned primaries into a 2PP. After 2004, Newspoll started using LEFs.

2.YouGov in 2025

YouGov handed down these primaries in its final poll before the 2025 election:

From these primaries it published a 2PP of 52.2, missing the result by −3.0. LEFs would have produced 53.9 from the same primaries, a miss of −1.3. Both figures come from one sample, so the 1.7-point gap between them is down to the choice of flows, not sampling luck. The −1.3 would have been as close as any final poll of 2025 came.

Unwise in retrospect, YouGov’s stated rationale for departing from LEFs was that Labor’s 2022 preferences reflected an unpopular incumbent, Scott Morrison, and with Labor now the incumbent, those flows could not be assumed to repeat.

3.Newspoll in 2019 and 2025: foreseeing One Nation’s drift towards the Coalition

In 2019, One Nation preferences hardened towards the Coalition, against Labor, by 14.7 points compared with the previous election. In 2025, they hardened a further 10.2 points.

In 2019, Newspoll saw this coming. It said:

The preference flow from Pauline Hanson’s One Nation at recent state elections has shifted substantially since the 2016 federal election when just 50.5 per cent of their preferences went to the Coalition. At the West Australian and Queensland elections the party did not preference against the sitting member and this resulted in the following preference flow from One Nation to the Liberal Party/LNP: WA (60.6 per cent [60.7% on this site]); Queensland (65.2 per cent [66.3% on this site]).

When we noted this shift in preferences we recalibrated the Newspoll preference flow allocation from One Nation to the Coalition from 50 to 60 per cent.

Newspoll’s published 2PP missed by +3.0 points in 2019, while its implied 2PP missed by +3.9. It still missed by a wide margin, about as much as the average pollster, but its departure from LEFs cut its error noticeably.

In 2025, Newspoll did the same thing. To quote:

Campbell White, head of Pyxis Polling & Insights which independently administers Newspoll for The Australian said: “Newspoll uses previous election flows to calculate two-party-preferred because it has consistently proved to be more reliable than other methods. However, based on what happened at the Queensland state election and through questions where we ask respondents about their intentions, it is clear that One Nation preferences are likely to flow more strongly to the Coalition than they did at the 2022 federal election.

“For this reason, we are adjusting the calculation of preferences accordingly.”

Again, here are the preference flows it had to work with:

Interestingly, although it correctly anticipated a further hardening of One Nation preferences towards the Coalition, Newspoll’s published 2PP (−2.7) did not outperform its implied 2PP (−2.2) in 2025. In other words, it would have done better simply using LEFs. This seems to be because, while the share of One Nation preferences reaching the Coalition rose (moving the election 2PP -0.7 points for Labor), the share of Greens and others’ preferences reaching the Coalition fell (moving it +1.0 points for Labor). The second shift more than offset the first, and Newspoll foresaw, and accounted for, only one of them.

The lesson is that strong evidence of one preference-flow shift does not necessarily justify displacing the LEF presumption, because other shifts may well offset it.

4.LEF misses: 2013, 2019, 2022

In 2013 and 2022, LEFs on the correct primaries produced errors of −1.04 and −0.87 respectively. Together with the famous industry failure of 2019 (+0.82), these are the big, clear misses of the last 40 years.3

Why did they miss? In 2013, preferences from the Greens and from all other minor parties hardened towards Labor, by 4.2 and 5.1 points respectively. The same happened in 2022: Greens preferences hardened by 3.5 points and others by 3.9.

In 2019, as Newspoll broadly anticipated, One Nation preferences hardened towards the Coalition by 14.7 points. At the same time, preferences from all other minor parties hardened in the same direction by 3.1 points.
