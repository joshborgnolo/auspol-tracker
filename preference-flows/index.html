Methods

The two-party figure, two ways

Above the chart, one line says where the site’s two-party figure comes from: “Using implied preference flows” or “Using respondent-allocated preferences only”. This page explains both: what each one is, why implied is the default, where its preference flows come from (including the Labor–One Nation flows no election has ever counted), and how polls become the headline figure.

In short Pollsters can’t observe preferences directly, so a two-party figure has to estimate how the final count would go. Each pollster does that its own way and publishes the result: that’s the published figure, usually built by asking respondents. This site also runs every poll’s primary votes through one fixed table, the flows counted at the 2025 election: that’s the implied figure. It’s the default because it moves only when voting intentions move, never because a pollster changed its method. Both are on the page, one click apart.

Why a two-party figure needs preferences at all

Australians vote by numbering candidates. When the last-placed candidate is excluded, each of their ballots moves to the voter’s next choice, and the count repeats until two candidates remain. The two-party preferred figure applies that arithmetic nationally: once every other candidate’s preferences are distributed, what share of the vote ends with Labor, and what share with the Coalition?

A poll measures the first step directly: the primary vote, who people put first. It can’t directly measure the second: where each minor-party and independent vote ends up. At the 2025 election a third of voters put someone other than Labor or the Coalition first; in 2026 polls it’s more than half. So the redistribution largely is the two-party figure. There are two ways to estimate it, and the basis switch chooses between them.

Two ways to the final count

Ask. The pollster asks respondents where their preferences would go, or in some methods works out an allocation from the sample, and publishes the result. That’s each pollster’s headline two-party figure, and what the toggle calls respondent-allocated. Some pollsters genuinely ask: Roy Morgan prints the figure its respondents produce every week, and every pollster publishing a Labor v One Nation figure builds it from respondents. Others lean on how preferences flowed at the last election: this term Newspoll’s Labor v Coalition figures have matched the 2025 flows to within rounding, and YouGov’s have tracked them closely. So this page uses the broader word published.

Assume. The alternative asks no one. Take the flows an actual election count measured (at the 2025 election, 88.2% of Greens preferences ended with Labor, and so on) and apply them, unchanged, to every poll’s primary votes. The assumption is fixed, open to inspection and the same for every pollster, so the result moves only when the primaries do. That’s the implied figure. A poll joins it whenever it publishes a full set of primaries that adds up; one missing a party, or with a documented error in its sums, stays out.

Roy Morgan publishes both every week: a respondent-allocated two-party figure and one on 2025-election flows. The site treats the respondent figure as Morgan’s published one, and uses its election-flows figure as a check in the drift chart described below. The “Compare” checkbox under the chart overlays the other basis on whichever one you’re viewing, so the two can be read against each other across the whole term.

Why implied is the default

The implied figure became the default on 18 September 2026. The hero, the table aggregates, and the share card all quote it, and the toggle marks it “(default)”. Five reasons, most important first:

  • Movement means mood A trend in published figures mixes two things: changes in how people intend to vote, and changes in how preferences are allocated, as pollsters enter, revise their assumptions or change method. The implied figure has only the first, because its allocation was fixed on election night. When it moves, the primaries moved.
  • It has the track record The idea isn’t new, and its record is long. Kevin Bonham’s track record of last-election preferences tests it at every federal election since 1983: applying the previous election’s flows to the final primary votes missed the actual two-party result by 0.3 points on average, and by a point or more only in 1990 and 2013 (Labor beat the projection both times). Asking has the worse history. In 2004 pollsters expected 69–74% of minor-party preferences to reach Labor; 61% did, and polls showing 50–50 became a 52.7 Coalition win. By Bonham’s arithmetic, matching the fixed table’s accuracy by asking would take about 15,000 minor-party respondents per election. The method does miss sometimes, as in 2019 (flows shifted 0.8 points to the Coalition) and 2022 (a point back to Labor). Those are level errors that surface on election night, and the drift chart described below exists to catch them early.
  • More polls feed it The implied figure needs only primary votes, which every poll publishes. The published figure needs a two-party number, which not every poll gives. In mid-September 2026 the three-week window for Labor v Coalition held 8 polls on the implied basis and 5 on the published. More polls make a steadier estimate.
  • One assumption, stated in full Quoting published figures across a dozen pollsters means quoting a dozen private compromises. The implied figure’s entire assumption is printed on this page: one table of flows, measured on 15 million ballots and applied identically to every pollster.
  • An honest anchor Every figure has to be pinned to something real. The published figure’s anchor is inside each pollster’s method. The implied figure’s is the only national measurement of the thing itself: the 2025 count.

What the published figure still offers: a fixed table can’t notice that a party’s supporters have shifted, but a pollster who asks can. So the published series stays complete beside the implied one, every pollster and every poll, and it would be first to show a real change in preference behaviour. Asking has a blind spot of its own, though. A respondent answers without a how-to-vote card in hand, while a counted flow includes everyone who followed one. That matters most for Labor v One Nation (below). At the 2025 election, the pollsters’ last-election-flows figures matched the count closely, and that’s the same method the implied figure uses.

The classic table: Labor v Coalition

For Labor v Coalition the implied figure needs no estimates of its own: the Electoral Commission counted the whole answer. Its 2025 two-party preference flow release distributes every formal ballot in all 150 divisions between Labor and the Coalition, and three lines give the site what it needs:

The Commission publishes the same election’s flows two other ways: a web table that includes ballots ending with third parties (Greens 79.9% to Labor), and a two-candidate download that collapses every destination to the two majors (86.8 / 27.1 / 48.5).

Three-cornered contests. Where both Coalition parties run against Labor, one is excluded first and its preferences are distributed. Most stay in the Coalition, but not all: of 11,176 Liberal ballots transferred, 12.47% went to Labor, and of 51,243 National ballots, 18.97%. That’s 11,117 ballots, or 0.072 points of the national two-party figure, that a Labor v Coalition formula gives to the Coalition when the ballots didn’t. The site adds it back as a flat constant. That’s the only honest shape for it, because the leak depends on how many seats are three-cornered, which a national poll can’t know. Kevin Bonham’s aggregate uses the same term, at 0.07.

The site uses the two-party cut because it measures exactly what the site simulates, every formal ballot ending with Labor or the Coalition, and because it performs better: it tracks Roy Morgan’s own published 2025-flows figure twice as closely as the alternatives, and it won the site’s constants review of 29 August 2026 on average pollster bias. Applied to the election’s own primaries, it reproduces the official 55.2–44.8 result by construction. That’s a built-in consistency check, not a forecasting claim.

For any poll, the implied figure is one line of arithmetic: Labor’s primary, plus each minor group’s primary times its flow, plus the small three-cornered term.

One cell matters most today: the share of One Nation preferences that reach Labor. With One Nation near 27% of the primary vote, each point of that flow is worth about a quarter of a point of two-party. So the chart shades a sensitivity bracket around the implied line: the same figure recalculated with the One Nation flow from the 2022 election, 35.7% to Labor. At today’s One Nation vote that bracket is close to three points wide, and it only runs towards Labor. If One Nation’s voters have changed since 2025, the evidence so far points the other way: many of them are former Coalition voters, and RedBridge’s respondents have sent fewer of their preferences to Labor as 2026 went on. So read the bracket as the price of that one assumption, not as its likely error.

The Labor v One Nation table: three flows that don’t officially exist

When pollsters began asking, late in 2025, how Labor would fare against One Nation head to head, they hit a gap in the official record. One Nation made the final two in only two of 150 seats in 2025 (Hunter against Labor, Maranoa against the LNP), so no national Labor v One Nation flow has ever been counted. The five pollsters publishing the pairing all ask their respondents, and their results disagree. Asking has a particular weakness here. Close to half of Coalition voters copy their party’s how-to-vote card, and in 2025 those cards put One Nation above Labor nearly everywhere. A respondent answers without the card, so asking probably overstates Labor; Kevin Bonham puts the effect at about two points. For this pairing above all, one fixed table that anyone can inspect beats asking. The site’s implied Labor v One Nation figure uses three settings, each built from the ballots that do exist:

How the Labor–One Nation table was built

  • Step one: start from the Senate ballots No 2025 House count had to compare Labor with One Nation, but every Senate ballot did the equivalent: each voter’s numbered list of parties shows which of the two they ranked higher. In the 2025 Senate formal-preferences release, 74.7% of Coalition ballots put One Nation ahead of Labor, as did 4.4% of Greens ballots and 50.9% of all others. That’s a count, not a model. But the Senate is a different chamber, with party lists instead of local candidates and a different mix of minor parties, so it’s where the table starts, not where it ends.
  • Step two: carry it into a House count The 2026 contests where One Nation made the final two show how a Senate ranking carries into House preferences. South Australia’s Liberal voters gave One Nation about 66%, against 70.6% on that state’s Senate ballots. Secret Harbour’s gave it something in the high 60s, against Western Australia’s 73.5%. Both readings are estimates from partial data (see below). They’re why the Coalition setting came down from a Senate-based 72% to 68.5% on 11 September 2026.
  • Step three: allow for the voters the contests missed A national figure needs voters neither contest included. Nationals voters lean further to One Nation: at the Farrer by-election 69% of Nationals preferences went to One Nation against 59% of Liberal ones, and Hunter’s 82.9% was a Nationals flow. Coalition voters in New South Wales and Queensland lean further than those in South Australia and Western Australia. Against that, both contests were fought where One Nation was strong, and neither includes city seats like the seven where the Coalition put Labor above One Nation in 2025. For the Greens and others, the comparison that matters is how each group treats One Nation against how it treats the Coalition, set out with each setting above. On 11 October 2026 those two settings moved: Greens from 89% to Labor to 91%, others from 53% to 55%.
  • Step four: cross-check, but never blend Hunter’s actual 2025 count (82.9% of Coalition preferences to One Nation), Kevin Bonham’s own table (71 · 9 · 45 to One Nation, behind his Labor v One Nation estimate), the Farrer by-election’s flows against an independent and the pollsters’ respondent splits are kept as checks the numbers must pass, not as inputs. Hunter was counted in One Nation’s strongest seat, and mixing heartland numbers into a national estimate is the one error the method is built to avoid. On today’s primaries Bonham’s table and the site’s give figures about half a point apart.

The 2026 contests

The pairing stopped being hypothetical in 2026. South Australia voted in March, and Labor and One Nation made the final two in 25 of its 47 seats. In August One Nation won the Secret Harbour by-election in Western Australia, its first lower-house seat in that state. Both contests ran under how-to-vote cards like the 2025 federal ones, with One Nation above Labor nearly everywhere. Unfortunately, neither produced a clean count of how each party’s own voters ranked Labor against One Nation, so both readings below are estimates:

The reconciliation. Against their own states’ Senate readings, both came in lower: South Australia about 66% to One Nation against 70.6%, Secret Harbour in the high 60s against 73.5%. That’s why the table no longer sits on the Senate figure (72% to One Nation until 11 September). It doesn’t simply average the two contests either. Both are readings of Liberal voters in the two big states least inclined to One Nation, so the national setting allows for the voters they leave out (step three above).

The distributions, seat by seat. Ben Raue’s Tally Room tabulation and Antony Green’s results analysis read the same South Australian distributions seat by seat. Where the Liberal candidate was the last excluded, about two-thirds of the bundle went to One Nation, within a point of Bonham’s statewide estimate. A bundle carries votes passed on from candidates excluded earlier, so it never cleanly reads one party’s own voters, and raw final-round flows exist only where One Nation ran strong. Green’s tables also hold the cleanest South Australian test of how Greens voters treat the two rivals: where their preferences split three ways, the same ballots favoured Labor by 89.8% against One Nation and 87.3% against the Liberals.

Bonham’s estimates also run the other way: against the Liberals, One Nation preferences went 27.2% to Labor, against 29.0% in South Australia at the 2025 federal election. His statewide Labor v One Nation “shadow two-party” came to 58.2, almost identical to his Labor v Liberal estimate of 57.9.

What respondents say. RedBridge has printed a respondent-allocated split by first preference every month since February 2026. Against One Nation, its Coalition voters have given Labor between 31% and 44% (a median of 32%), its Greens voters 89–92%, and its other voters 57–70%. In February Newspoll asked the same question with a “don’t know or follow the card” option: Liberal voters split 43% One Nation, 33% Labor and 24% unsure, and Nationals voters 70%, 6% and 24%. If all the unsure follow their card, the Coalition gives Labor about 28%; if half do, about 32%. Respondent splits are noisy, with about 200 Coalition voters a month, and they miss card-following, so they check the table rather than set it. They don’t always lean to Labor, either: in South Australia, YouGov’s and Fox & Hedgehog’s Labor v One Nation figures landed slightly below what the estimated flows give on their own primary votes.

Kept as checks, not inputs. Hunter’s 2025 count (82.9 · 12.6 · 59.8 to One Nation) is the only 2025 federal seat where voters actually made this choice, and it was One Nation’s strongest seat: its share there was 25.7%, against 14.6% statewide. Every number from it is heartland, not national. Bonham’s January 2026 reconstruction of the same Senate data (about 72 · 9 · 45, since trimmed to 71% on the Coalition) is the independent check on the anchor. Farrer’s May 2026 by-election measured a different pairing, One Nation against an independent, so its flows (59% of Liberal and 69% of Nationals preferences to One Nation) answer a different question, though the gap between the two parties’ voters carries over. Maranoa tells the same story from the other side: in One Nation’s other heartland seat, 35% of Greens and 68% of other preferences still reached One Nation, far above any national figure, just as a second heartland seat would predict.

What the table would have meant in 2025

Apply the current table to the actual 2025 first preferences (Labor 34.6, Coalition 31.8, Greens 12.2, One Nation 6.4, others 15.0) and the recorded 55.2–44.8 Labor v Coalition result becomes a Labor v One Nation figure of about 64–36, with a range of roughly 62–66:

ALP 34.6 + .315×31.8 + .91×12.2 + .55×15.0 = 64.0 ON 6.4 + .685×31.8 + .09×12.2 + .45×15.0 = 36.0 Labor–One Nation two-party ≈ 64.0 – 36.0 (band ±1.9)

That’s arithmetic on 2025 votes, not a forecast. One Nation has polled at several times its 2025 vote through 2026, and the 2026 contests are the first evidence of what preferences do at those levels. The site runs the same sum on every new poll: the implied Labor v One Nation figure is the current polls’ primaries read through this table.

From polls to the headline figure

Whichever basis you’re viewing, the headline is built from individual polls the same way, and the line under the figure, such as “Weighted aggregate (9 polls over 21 days as at 14 Sep; 95% interval)”, says how:

  • The weighted aggregate Each poll from the past three weeks is weighted by sample size and by recency (its weight halves every seven days and fades smoothly to zero at the window’s edge), after removing its pollster’s measured lean: each pollster’s habit of running a little above or below the others is estimated and subtracted first. Where too few pollsters ask a contest for those leans to be measured, the label says “Monthly average” instead, the figure is a plain average of the month’s polls, and a “Limited data” tag appears.
  • The ± on the lead is double the ± on each share The 95% interval on a two-party figure is measured on one party’s share. The lead line, as in “Labor leads by 3.0 ± 2 pts”, doubles it: the two shares add to 100, so any error that raises one lowers the other by the same amount, and the gap between them moves twice as far. Beside the arrow showing the change since a month ago, “within the margin” appears when that change is smaller than its own interval, so it isn’t a finding.
  • …and Labor v One Nation carries a wider margin The implied Labor v One Nation figure has two doubts: the polls’ own sampling error, and doubt about the flow table itself (the bands above, ±3 / ±2.5 / ±4, applied to today’s primaries). The site adds them as independent errors and calls the total a margin: today about ±4.4 on the lead, ±3.4 from the polls and ±2.8 from the flows. The chart’s tooltip and the “?” beside the figure name both parts. It isn’t called a 95% interval, because the table’s band is a judgement about flows no count has checked, not a sampling probability. Until 11 October 2026 the site quoted the table’s band alone, which left the polls’ sampling error out.
  • Which contest leads The page leads with Labor’s main rival, judged on the implied figures in two steps. A rival that runs Labor clearly closer after preferences, by more than that gap’s own margin, takes the spot. That margin is mostly doubt about the One Nation flow table, because the polls’ sampling error largely cancels when both contests come from the same polls. When the two contests are too close to call, the rival clearly ahead on first preferences leads, since it would face Labor in more seats. That has been One Nation since February 2026: after preferences the two contests have stayed within about a point of each other, inside a margin of about 1.5, while One Nation has led the Coalition on first preferences by 2 to 10 points.

Two checks watch the implied figure itself. The sensitivity bracket on the Labor v Coalition line re-prices the One Nation flow at the last two counted elections. And the preference-flow drift chart compares each poll’s published figure with what its primaries imply under the fixed table, pooling the gaps across the term after adjusting for each pollster’s lean. That’s the alarm that would show the 2025 table going stale. A fixed table can’t track real changes in preferences, and isn’t meant to. When it fails, it fails as a level error the drift chart measures openly, not as a phantom movement it quietly invents.

Where each basis can go wrong

The implied figure adds no information about preferences. Same primaries, same table, same answer, every time. It can’t discover that One Nation voters have changed their second choices; it can only assume they haven’t. Election night, not the table, is the final test.

Time works against a fixed table. The 2025 count measured 2025. As the term goes on, the implied figure can drift from the truth while looking perfectly steady. That’s why the published series is one click away and the drift chart runs permanently.

The Labor v One Nation settings rest on thin evidence. Two state-level contests, both read from partial data, and one heartland federal seat are the whole lower-house record of that pairing so far, and none is a national contest. The Senate ballots are plentiful, but they’re a different chamber with a different “others” pool, where a tenth to a fifth of ballots never reach either party. The next real tests are South Australia’s flows by first preference, due late in 2026, and Victoria’s election in November.

Everything rests on today’s how-to-vote cards. About 40–45% of Coalition voters follow their card. If the Liberals put Labor above One Nation, the Coalition setting could move by 15 points or more, and the figure by 3 points or more towards Labor. Smaller switches matter too: Queensland’s 2019 count shows One Nation’s own card moving its voters’ flow by about eleven points.

The published figure isn’t a pure reading either. Pollsters allocate preferences differently, change methods mid-term, and a few allocate by last-election flows themselves, which is this site’s implied method, applied privately and unlabelled. Read “respondent-allocated” as “what the pollsters publish”, read the implied figure for movement, and keep both in view.
