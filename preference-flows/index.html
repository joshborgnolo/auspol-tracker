The two-party figure, two ways

Above the chart, one small sentence says where the site’s two-party number comes from — “Using implied preference flows”, or “Using respondent-allocated preferences only”. The “?” beside it leads here. This page is the whole story: what each version is, why the implied one is the default, where its fixed preference flows were measured — including the Labor–One Nation flows that no election has ever counted — and how polls become the headline number.

In one breath A two-party figure simulates the final count, and pollsters cannot observe preferences directly. Each house solves that its own way and publishes the result — that is the published figure, and asking respondents is its dominant method. This site also re-allocates every poll’s primary votes through one frozen flow table, the 2025 election’s own count — that is the implied figure, and the default, because its movements come only from how people say they will vote, never from a change of allocation method. Both stay on the page, a click apart.

Why a two-party figure needs preferences at all

Australia votes by numbering candidates. When the count eliminates the last-placed candidate, every ballot moves onward to whoever its voter ranked next, and the count repeats until one candidate remains. The two-party preferred figure applies that same arithmetic nationally: of every formal vote, what share would end with Labor and what share with the Coalition once all the others’ preferences have arrived where they are going.

A poll can measure the first half of that directly — the primary vote, what people write as their number one. What it cannot measure directly is the second half: where each minor-party and independent vote finishes. Roughly a third of the vote today is cast for someone other than Labor or the Coalition, so the redistribution is the two-party figure. There are only two ways to fill that gap, and the site’s basis switch is the choice between them.

Two ways to the final count

Ask. The pollster asks voters where their preferences would go — or in some houses’ methods, works out an allocation from the sample — and publishes the result. This is the figure each house ships as its headline two-party, and it is what the toggle calls respondent-allocated: for the big established houses (Roy Morgan, Newspoll and others), the answer genuinely comes out of the questionnaire. Different houses still blend in different ingredients — some ask outright, some lean partly on how preferences flowed at the last election — so “published” is the honest umbrella word and the one this page uses.

Assume. The alternative never asks anyone. Take the flow table that an actual election count measured — at the 2025 election, 88.2 per cent of Greens preferences ended with Labor, and so on — and apply it, unchanged, to every poll’s primary votes. The assumption is pinned, inspectable and identical for every house; the resulting figure can only move when the primaries themselves move. That is the implied figure, and a poll joins it whenever it publishes a full primary set whose columns add up — a poll missing a party column, or with a documented anomaly in its sums, stays out.

One house straddles the line transparently: Roy Morgan publishes both bases every week — a respondent-allocated two-party and a figure on 2025-election flows. The site files Morgan’s respondent figure as the published one and keeps its election-flows figure for the drift watch described below. The “Compare” switch under the chart does a similar thing for any house: it overlays the other basis’s line without switching your view, so the two can be read against each other at every point in the term.

Why implied is the default

The implied basis became the site’s default two-party figure on 18 September 2026 — the hero, the table aggregates and the share card all quote it, and the toggle’s implied side labels itself “(default)”. Four reasons, in declining order of importance:

  • Movement means mood A trend in published two-party figures mixes two things: how voters’ intentions are changing, and how the methods that allocate preferences are changing — a new house enters the mix, an old one revises its assumptions, an allocation regime shifts. The implied figure has only the first ingredient, because its allocation was frozen on election night. When it moves, primaries moved.
  • More polls feed it The implied series only needs primary votes, which every poll publishes; the published series needs a two-party figure, which not every poll publishes. As this page is written (September 2026), the classic pairing’s three-week estimation window holds nine polls on the implied basis against six on the published — and for Labor v One Nation it is nine against five. A denser window means a steadier estimate.
  • One assumption, stated in full Quoting levels across a dozen houses means quoting a dozen private compromises. The implied figure’s entire assumption is printed on this page — one table of flow shares, measured on 15 million ballots — and every house faces the identical arithmetic. Comparability wave-to-wave and house-to-house is bought with a single, named choice.
  • The level must come from somewhere honest Any figure needs an anchor: something real that pins today’s estimate to a measured level. The published basis finds its anchor inside each house’s method. The implied basis uses the only national measurement of the thing itself — the 2025 count.

What the published basis still buys: a frozen table cannot notice that how-to-vote cards have changed or that a party’s supporters have drifted, but a pollster who asks can. The published series therefore stays complete beside the implied one — every house, every wave — and it is the basis that gets to say “yes” first if real preference behaviour ever moves far from the table. It has also done its job before: at the 2025 election the published last-election-flows figures matched the count closely, and the table the implied basis runs is exactly that tradition — an election’s own flows, re-used.

For the traditional pairing the implied figure needs no estimates of its own: the Electoral Commission counted the entire answer at the election. The Labor v One Nation table: three flows that do not officially exist

When pollsters began asking, late in 2025, how Labor would fare against One Nation head-to-head, the question landed on a gap in the official record. One Nation made the final two-candidate count in only two of 150 electorates in 2025 — Hunter against Labor, Maranoa against the LNP — so no national flow between Labor and One Nation has ever been counted, and every house publishing the pairing has had to invent its own allocation. The levels those private inventions produce disagree with each other; there is no count to discipline them. For this pairing, more than any other, one frozen and inspectable assumption outranks an industry of private ones. The site’s implied Labor v One Nation figure runs on three measured-then-calibrated flows:

How the Labor–One Nation table was found

  • Step one — anchor on 7.4 million Senate ballots No 2025 House count had to measure Labor against One Nation, but every Senate ballot did something equivalent: each voter’s numbered list of parties records which group, Labor’s or One Nation’s, they ranked earlier. Counting that on the 2025 formal-preferences release gives the anchor: among Coalition ballots 74.7 per cent put One Nation earlier, among Greens 4.4, among all other parties 50.9 — 7.43 million ranked ballots in all. No model, no weighting: a count.
  • Step two — move the anchor to the House, then to reality The Senate count is corrected for the two chambers’ known differences (the ACT ran no One Nation Senate group; the “others” pool is 48 per cent independents in the House but party tickets in the Senate). Then the pairing’s own 2026 counts take over: South Australia in March, Secret Harbour in August. Where the real counts and the corrected anchor disagree, the table follows the counts — the Coalition cell moved from a Senate-anchored 72 down to 68.5 on 11 September 2026 precisely because two full counts of real ballots both said the Senate read ran high.
  • Step three — cross-check, but never blend Hunter’s actual 2025 count (82.9 Coalition to One Nation), Kevin Bonham’s independent reconstruction (~72), and the Farrer by-election’s flows against an independent stay as checks the numbers must survive, not ingredients. Hunter’s count was taken in One Nation’s strongest seat; blending heartland numbers into a national estimate is the one error the construction is built to avoid.

The counts that actually happened, 2026

The pairing stopped being hypothetical the year after the election. South Australia voted in March 2026 and the Labor–One Nation count ran statewide; in August the Secret Harbour by-election in Western Australia produced another full count — and One Nation won, its first state lower-house seat there. Both counts ran under the same how-to-vote regime as the 2025 federal election: One Nation carded above Labor nearly everywhere, so the calibration transfers like-for-like — and a future decision to put Labor first would move everything (Queensland’s 2019 count prices such a card flip at about eleven points).

The reconciliation. Read against their own states’ Senate anchors, both real Coalition counts came in low — South Australia 66.1 to One Nation against 70.6 (−4.5); Secret Harbour 70.2 against 73.5 (−3.3). An early construction kept the corrected Senate anchor with a partial discount (72 to One Nation); the shipped figure accepts what the two full counts say and anchors between them at 68.5 to One Nation (31.5 to Labor), band 66–71 to straddle both counts and the anchor’s edge. The two Greens counts, 10.0 and 15.2 to One Nation, centre on 11. And South Australia’s independent count — 44.1 to One Nation — landed inside the others band, which is left unchanged at 47. South Australia also ran the reciprocal count: against the Liberals, its One Nation preferences went 27.2 to Labor (the 2025 federal figure: 25.5), and its statewide Labor–One Nation “shadow two-party” came to 58.2 — all but identical to its real Labor–Liberal 57.9.

Kept as checks, not ingredients. Hunter’s 2025 count (82.9 · 12.6 · 59.8 to One Nation) is the only 2025 federal electorate where voters actually made this choice — in One Nation’s strongest seat, where its run-off share ran at 25.7 per cent against 14.6 statewide; every number there is heartland, not nation. Bonham’s January 2026 reconstruction of the same Senate data (~72 · ~9 · ~45) is the independent pair of eyes the anchor is checked against. Farrer’s May 2026 by-election measured a different final pairing — One Nation against an independent — and its 59.0 Liberal-to-One-Nation flow belongs to that question, not this one. Maranoa tells the matching side-story: in One Nation’s other heartland seat, 35 per cent of Greens and 68 per cent of other preferences still found One Nation — far above any national figure, exactly as a second heartland seat would predict.

What the table would have meant in 2025

Apply the shipped cells to the actual 2025 first preferences — Labor 34.6, Coalition 31.8, Greens 12.2, One Nation 6.4, others 15.0 — and the recorded 55.2–44.8 Labor–Coalition result becomes a Labor–One Nation figure of about 63–37, with the band’s honest range roughly 62–65:

ALP 34.6 + .315×31.8 + .89×12.2 + .53×15.0 = 63.4 ON 6.4 + .685×31.8 + .11×12.2 + .47×15.0 = 36.6 Labor–One Nation two-party ≈ 63.4 – 36.6 (band ±1.6)

That is arithmetic on 2025 votes, not a forecast: One Nation has spent 2026 polling at several times its 2025 vote, and what preferences do at those primary levels is precisely what the 2026 counts are the first answers to. The same exercise is re-run live on the site every week: the implied Labor v One Nation figure is the current polls’ primaries read through this table.

From polls to the headline number

Whichever basis you are looking at, the headline is built from individual polls the same way, and the sentence under the figure — “Weighted aggregate (9 polls over 21 days as at 14 Sep; 95% interval)” — is its provenance:

  • The weighted aggregate Each poll in the trailing three weeks is weighted by its sample size, by recency (a poll’s weight halves every seven days, fading smoothly to zero at the window’s edge), and against the house’s own measured lean — each firm’s habit of running a little above or below the cross-house consensus is estimated and removed before pooling. When too few polls are in the window the label says “Monthly average” and the figure carries a “limited data” tag instead: the plain, unweighted reading of the month’s polls.
  • The interval is a share, doubled on the lead line The “95% interval” attached to a two-party figure is measured on one party’s share. The line that reads “Labor leads by 3.0 ± 2 pts” doubles it, because a lead is the difference of two shares and a difference carries squared error of its own. If the gap between the parties is smaller than that doubled figure, the site says “within the margin” beside the arrow rather than implying a meaningful move.
  • …except “flows range”, which is something else entirely Under Labor v One Nation the interval is the flow table’s own band — the ±2.5/±3/±3 above — read through today’s primaries, not a sampling interval at all. It answers “how far would this figure move if the table’s best cells were at either edge of their band?”. It is deliberately never called a 95% interval: that phrase belongs to procedures a real count can check, and this pairing has no count.

Two diagnostics watch the implied basis itself. The sensitivity bracket on the classic line reprices the One Nation conversion cell at the last two counted elections. And the preference-flow drift tracker holds each poll’s published figure against what its primaries imply under the frozen table, pooling the gap house-adjusted across the term — that is the tripwire that would announce the 2025 table going stale. A frozen table cannot track real preference drift; it was never asked to. Its failure mode is a level error the drift panel measures out loud, not a phantom movement it silently invents.

Where each basis can go wrong

The implied figure adds no information about preferences. Same primaries, same table, same answer — always. It cannot discover that One Nation supporters have changed their second choices, only assume they have not. Election night, not the flow table, remains the final examiner.

Time is the frozen table’s enemy. The 2025 count was a measurement of 2025. As the term ages, the implied level can drift from the truth while looking perfectly behaved — which is exactly why the published series sits one click away and the drift tracker runs permanently.

The Labor–One Nation cells rest on thin real evidence. A state election, a by-election and one heartland federal seat are the entire lower-house record of that pairing to date — none at a neutral national scene. The Senate anchor is huge but a different chamber with a different “others” pool, where a tenth to a fifth of ballots stop at one group and drop out of the comparison entirely. The how-to-vote card regime is baked into every measured number, and cards can change.

Nor is published a pure read. Houses allocate preferences differently, methods change mid-term, and a few allocate by last-election flow themselves — which is this site’s implied basis, privately applied and unlabelled. Treat “respondent-allocated” as “what the houses ship”, read the implied basis for movement, and keep both on the table.
