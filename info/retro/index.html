Answers to questions you may have

What things mean, how things are built, how to read them, and their limitations. Each section has its own explainer, and where applicable, the formulas and working are shown for transparency.

auspol tracker is an unofficial aggregator of Australian federal political polls. It collects published polls, adjusts each for its pollster’s lean, weights them by recency and sample size, and pools them into aggregate estimates. To be clear, the site runs no polls of its own; it only gathers and presents those published by others. It aims to cut through the noise and give a clear view of Australian political sentiment: how Australians intend to vote, and their views on leaders, issues, and our country’s direction.

Updates. New polls are added as they are released, mostly by automated checks but also manually by me (some guy). Every figure is rebuilt when a new poll lands.

Following along. The RSS feed lists each new poll as it is added, with a link to the pollster’s own release. The change log records what has moved the two-party preferred estimate, each statistical finding and the economic-confidence gauges, entry by entry.

Feedback. Spot a wrong number or have any other feedback? Let me know.

Archives. Older federal polling archives are kept here for safekeeping.

What am I looking at?

In the main chart, each dot is one published poll, placed at the midpoint of its fieldwork. The line is a monthly average of the polls, weighted by sample size and adjusted for each pollster’s lean, with its 95% interval shaded around it. Where two bands overlap, the polls can’t separate the parties that month. The line’s points sit mid-month, so late in a month the newest dots run past its end, though they’re already counted in it. The figure at the top comes from the last three weeks of polls (see Weighted aggregate), so it moves with every poll. Other charts follow the same pattern. A dash (—) in a table means the pollster didn’t ask that question.

Where do the numbers come from?

From the pollsters: every national voting-intention poll published since the May 2025 election, from these 13 houses, most polls first: Roy Morgan, YouGov, Newspoll, RedBridge/Accent, Resolve, DemosAU, Essential, Spectre Strategy, Freshwater, Fox & Hedgehog, Wolf & Smith, EMRS (Tas), Agenda C Synesis. A pollster’s MRP or SMS polls count under its own name. All polls files each one with its fieldwork dates, sample size, a link to the release and, where one exists, its APC statement. Past cycles holds every term back to 1972.

How is the headline worked out?

It averages the last three weeks of polls, counting newer and larger polls for more, after taking out each pollster’s usual lean (see Weighted aggregate). Rather than average the pollsters’ own two-party figures, each worked out a different way, the two-party figure reads every poll’s primary votes through one fixed preference table (see Implied 2PP). The headline follows the rival Labor is doing worse against: today the Coalition, at 50.9–49.1.

How accurate are the polls?

The 95% interval says how far chance alone could put a figure off: ±1.1 points on each party’s share for Labor against the Coalition today. Past elections show the rest. The How the final polls have done panel in Past cycles scores each pollster’s last two-party figure in the 14 days before polling day against the result. Across the 24 elections from 1946 to 2025 with final polls on record (none survive for 1955 or 1958 or 1961 or 1963 or 1966 or 1969 or 1990), the final polls missed by 1.4 points on average, and by 3.2 at worst, in 1977. In 2019 all 5 houses missed on the same side, by 2.9. Unfortunately, no aggregate can see an error every pollster shares, which is why the interval never claims to cover it.

Is it a forecast? What would it mean in seats?

No to both. Everything here describes where opinion stands now, from polls already published, and final polls have missed past results by 1.4 points on average. Turning a national figure into seats would assume the swing is the same everywhere. With One Nation near 26% of the primary vote, that fails in exactly the seats that would decide the election: a large minor party wins where its vote is concentrated, and a national figure can’t see where that is. Where a pollster modelled seats one by one, its poll carries the MRP tag in All polls.

Why lead with two-party preferred?

Because seats are decided between the final two candidates. Preferences are distributed until one candidate has a majority, so “who’s ahead” is a two-party question that first preferences alone can’t answer. With One Nation near 26% of the primary vote, the two views of the race are far apart, so the primary vote has its own section.

Why doesn’t the newest poll match the headline?

Usually neither is wrong. A poll is one pollster’s reading of one week, and chance alone can put it off by its margin of error, so two honest polls of an unchanged electorate will differ too. The headline pools every recent poll after removing each pollster’s house effect, its habit of leaning one way. One poll moves it by its weight, not its news value: its aggregate effect shows how much. Poll disagreement tests whether the polls differ by more than chance allows.

What are the figures in the More menu?

Each page there is listed under the headline it opens with, and beside it the number that headline rests on, as the page prints it. For Poll disagreement, how widely the polls of the party they disagree on most are spread, as a multiple of what chance alone would produce. For Pollster leans, the lean the headline names, in points, with + towards Labor. For Respondent-allocated preference flows, the move the headline reports. A headline that reports no single number has none beside it.

Weighted aggregate.

How the headline and every other current figure are built. It averages the recent polls, giving more weight to newer and larger ones, after removing each pollster’s house effect.Recency. A poll’s weight halves every seven days, and it fades out over its third week, leaving the window on day 21. So the figure can move on a day with no new poll, as newer polls take over from older ones.Size. Larger samples count for more. Where a pollster publishes an effective sample, that figure is used. Otherwise the raw sample is discounted by 1.6, roughly what weighting costs the pollsters that do publish, and capped at 3,000.Repeat polls. A pollster with several polls in the window counts for the square root of their number, so three weekly Roy Morgan polls count as 1.7, not 3.Exclusions. These stay in the archive but count towards no aggregate. Roy Morgan’s SMS polls have a strong selection bias. Releases that combine a pollster’s polls which already count would count those voters twice: Newspoll’s quarterly tables, Roy Morgan’s seven-month release, and DemosAU’s 2026 models (MRP), whose fieldwork contains DemosAU’s own regular polls and which are taken to reuse their respondents. EMRS’s polls are of Tasmania only, and RedBridge’s March 2026 super-poll published breakdowns but no national figures.Sparse measures. Measures polled about once a week or less use a six-week window: favourability, Hanson’s approval, preferred prime minister, the One Nation split, the vote by group, the issues, and decidedness. There a poll’s weight halves every 14 days.Monthly lines. Each chart’s line uses the same method without the recency weighting: one point per calendar month, from that month’s polls. Past cycles draws every earlier term the same way. A few measures depart from all this, and their sections say how.

House effect.

A pollster’s consistent lean relative to the others. Each of its polls is compared with other pollsters’ polls taken around the same time, and the gaps are pooled with a 90-day half-life, so the lean tracks the pollster’s current methods. A lean measured from few polls is shrunk toward zero, as if joined by one and a half more polls sitting exactly on the others: a pollster with one recent poll keeps 40% of its measured lean, one with four keeps 73%, and one with ten keeps 87%. That setting best predicts each new poll from the polls before it. Each figure gets its own lean, since a pollster that leans to Labor on the two-party figure need not on a primary vote. The aggregate subtracts the lean as it stood at the time of each figure. The Pollster leans page, in the More menu, shows each pollster’s house lean.
Show the workingA pollster’s lean is Σvⱼgⱼ ÷ (Σvⱼ + 1.5) over its polls, where gⱼ is a poll’s gap to the sample-weighted average of other pollsters’ polls within 28 days of it (at least three of them), and vⱼ = 2^(−a/90), with a the poll’s age in days when the lean is read.

Monthly average.

What a contest gets when too few pollsters ask it to measure their house effects: a plain average of each month’s polls, with no adjustments. The two-party section says which kind it is showing, so an unadjusted average is never mistaken for the adjusted one.

95% interval.

How far a figure could plausibly be off by chance. For the two-party figure against the Coalition today, that’s ±1.1 points on each party’s share, and ±2.2 on the lead: anything that moves one share moves the gap between them twice as far. The interval takes the larger of two measures: how much the polls in the window disagree, and how much chance alone would scatter them if they all agreed. So it widens when the pollsters split, and never claims more precision than their samples allow.Unfortunately, it can’t see an error every poll shares. When the whole industry leans one way, as all 5 houses did in 2019, the interval has no way to know.

Here p̂ is the aggregate’s own share, and 1.6 is the design effect of a weighted panel.

Margin of error.

How far a single poll can miss purely because it surveyed a sample rather than everyone. A thousand respondents carry about ±3 points at 95% confidence, and it takes four times the interviews to halve that. It covers chance and nothing else: skewed samples, turnout assumptions and a pollster’s methods sit outside it. Pooling polls narrows the chance part, which is why the two-party 95% interval (±1.1 today) is tighter than one poll’s ±3. The part every pollster shares doesn’t shrink at all. The implied Labor v One Nation figure’s margin adds one more doubt, about its flow table, so it is wider than the polls’ alone.

Effective sample.

What a sample is worth once weighting is counted. A pollster’s APC statement gives one for each poll, shown as neff beside its sample. The window has one too: today the two-party window’s 9 polls are worth 6.3, since older polls, smaller samples and repeat polls from one pollster count for less. The 95% interval is computed from it.

Significant.

A difference or change that chance is an unlikely explanation for: if nothing had really differed or moved, a gap as large would turn up less than one time in twenty. Two figures differ significantly when the gap between them is larger than their margins combined. Figures from different people have errors that partly offset, so margins of 1.3 and 1.2 points combine to 1.8, not 2.5.Several at once. Test enough gaps at one in twenty and one will pass by chance, so the bar rises with the number tested. With six gaps, the clearest must pass at one in 120, the next at one in 100, then 80, and so on, stopping at the first that falls short.Over time. A change is a straight line through the readings, tested against how far they scatter around it. Each section says which readings it fits.Limits. One in twenty is a convention, and a result near the line can flip with one new poll. Across every tab, party and period, about one sentence in twenty could report a difference or change that isn’t there.
Show the workingGap between two figures: z = 1.96 × (a − b) ÷ √(±a² + ±b²), where a and b are the figures and ±a and ±b their 95% margins. p is the chance of a z at least that far from zero under the normal curve. p < 0.05 is the same as the gap exceeding √(±a² + ±b²).Several gaps: Holm’s method. With m gaps, sort their p from smallest. The kth smallest passes if p < 0.05 ÷ (m − k + 1) and every smaller one passed.

What are the ▲ and ▼ figures measured against?

It depends on where they sit.
  • Beside a current figure: the change on a month ago, from the same estimate built from the polls of 30 days earlier. It counts as a change only when it is larger than its own margin, which combines both readings’ intervals; otherwise the figure says “within the margin”.
  • On the primary vote figures: the change since the 2025 election, where every line on that chart begins.
  • In a poll’s breakdown and the poll tables: the change on that pollster’s own previous poll.
  • In the issues table by group: a group that differs from all voters by more than its own 95% margin.
Most say what they are measured against beside them, or when you point at them.

Each party’s share after every other candidate’s preferences have been distributed between the final two. It’s the figure that decides seats. The section shows Labor against the Coalition and against One Nation, and leads with the one Labor is doing worse against: the Coalition until June 2026, One Nation since. A contest no fixed table covers, such as the Coalition against One Nation, is shown as published.Published figures are averaged as Labor’s share of the two. Essential leaves undecided voters inside its pair, which falls short of 100 by that share; averaged as published, it would mix a share of everyone with a share of the decided and give Essential a false lean. Rescaling splits the undecided in proportion, only because a pair on its own gives no basis for splitting them any other way. The shortfall is kept as Essential’s own line in Decidedness. Implied 2PP.

The site’s main two-party figure, on the hero, the share card and the summary. It takes each poll’s primary votes and distributes the minor parties’ shares using one fixed preference table: against the Coalition, the flows counted at the 2025 election. Every pollster allocates preferences its own way, so one table for all of them means the figure moves when voters do, not when the mix of pollsters changes. It also counts every poll that publishes primaries, including those with no two-party figure of their own.Neither it nor the published figure is the true 2PP. Against the Coalition today, the implied figure reads 50.9 and the published 48.9. With One Nation on 26% of the primary vote, five points of doubt about where its preferences go moves the two-party figure 1.3 points either way.Labor v One Nation uses a different table, the Labor v One Nation flows, because no election has ever counted that pairing: 50.8 to Labor, 49.2 to One Nation, ±2.0 once doubt about that table is added to the polls’ own margin.Past cycles reads each earlier term through the flows of the election that opened it (see Last-election flows). Preference flows.

How minor-party votes split between the final two candidates once preferences are distributed. The implied 2PP against the Coalition uses the flows counted at the 2025 election (Greens 88.2%, One Nation 25.5% and all others 54.5% to Labor). The party-by-party history is charted in the preference flows explainer — flows exist only from 1996, when full preference data was first published, and One Nation’s line breaks where it barely stood candidates. Labor v One Nation uses its own Labor v One Nation flows, estimated because no election has counted that pairing.Are they still flowing that way? The Respondent-allocated preference flows page, in the More menu, checks two ways, one contest at a time. It sets what each party’s voters tell pollsters, when asked to choose between the final two, beside the flows the site assumes. And it watches the pollsters’ own two-party figures, which come largely from where their respondents say their preferences would go: if those drift from where the pollster’s primary votes and the 2025 flows put them, voters may be sending preferences differently. Each pollster is measured against its own habits: its polls in the six months after the election, or its first polls if it started later. With no counted Labor v One Nation flows, that chart shows drift since each pollster’s first head-to-heads. The check corrects no other figure.

Counted and estimated flows.

The chart in the preference flows explainer tracks the share of each group’s ballots that ended with Labor rather than the Coalition, election by election.Federal: counted. The Australian Electoral Commission follows every ballot to the final two and publishes where each party’s voters ended up. From 1996 to 2001 that count covers the seats where Labor and the Coalition were the final two, 142 to 145 of the 148 to 150. Since 2004 it covers every seat, nationally and by state: the Commission sorts every ballot between Labor and the Coalition, even where someone else made the final two. The seat-type and socio-economic views below take one estimated step.Seat types and socio-economic fifths. The seat-type views group each seat by the Commission’s own classification at that election (inner metro, outer metro, provincial or rural), so a seat that was reclassified moves with it. The 1996 and 1998 ratings come from the Commission’s whole-chamber tables of those years, 2001’s from its division profiles (three unrated seats from its research report) and 2004’s from its profile pages. The fifths rank every seat on one of three ABS indexes: IRSAD (advantage and disadvantage), IEO (education and occupation) or IER (economic resources). The most-advantaged 30 seats form the top fifth, and so on down; in the 151-seat chambers of 2019 and 2022 the spare seat goes to the bottom fifth, and in 1998’s 148-seat chamber the bottom fifth holds 28. IEO and IER start in 1998, from the Parliamentary Library’s 1996-census figures for that election’s seats; IRSAD starts in 2001. From 2004 to 2019 each seat’s percentile is interpolated between the censuses either side of election day; other years use the census ranking published for that election’s boundaries. Each election is ranked afresh, so a seat whose standing changed can change fifths. A whole seat sits in one fifth by its average, so a seat that mixes rich and poor suburbs counts entirely in one.Seats where a crossbencher made the final two. Before 2004 these seats are left out, and the points are drawn as hollow rings. From 2004 the Commission’s state table of flows by party, less the seats where Labor and the Coalition were the final two, gives each party’s flow across a state’s crossbench seats. Where those seats all sit in one seat type or fifth, that is a count. Where they are spread across several, the split between groups is fitted to three counts: each seat’s first preferences by party, each seat’s minor-party ballots that ended with Labor (its two-party Labor total less Labor’s own first preferences), and each party’s state total. Labor v One Nation flows.

The preference table behind the Labor v One Nation figure. No election has ever counted that contest, so the site estimates the table from the ballots that do exist. Three settings do all the work: Coalition voters give 27% to Labor (±4), Greens 91% (±2.5) and everyone else 55% (±4). Preferred prime minister.

Approval.

A rating of how a leader is doing the job: the share who approve minus the share who disapprove, called net approval. Favourability.

A rating of the leader as a person: favourable minus unfavourable. Each poll says how far a group sits from that poll’s own overall figure: One Nation ten points lower among 18–34s, say. Because no two pollsters draw the same income brackets, the Income tab places each bracket where it sits in the electorate – an under-$50k cut is roughly the poorest quarter, from the census income distribution – and fits each poll a line through its brackets, each weighted by how many voters it holds. The note by the chart names each house’s own end brackets.Reading a gap. Each figure carries its 95% margin, usually 2 to 5 points, with each poll’s sample for a group estimated from the group’s share of adults (18–34s are about 28%). The sentences call a difference significant only when it passes that test, with the bar raised when three or more groups are compared. They report what passes: a steady rise or fall across ordered groups such as age (only when every step passes), one group apart from the others, or failing those, the largest gap that passes. If no two groups differ, they name any group that sits clear of all voters by more than its margin, and only if none does, no difference.The headline. It names a group only when the group’s figure sits clear of all voters’ by more than its margin, and names each trait at most once each way. On a scale such as age or education, a lean at both ends is one fact: a party with older voters is named for them, not also for its missing young ones. A gap that stops short of the far end is a fact of its own, and states, which have no order, are named both ways. The age bands lead the generations. A generation replaces its band only when its gap is clearly the larger, and when the two disagree, age is left out. Up to five claims make the headline: those that most change the make-up of the party’s voters.Changes over time. The sentence under each chart asks whether a group has moved towards or away from the party, relative to all voters. The point-gap alone can’t say. When a party grows everywhere, a group keeping its usual share of the party’s vote sees its gap widen by arithmetic alone: a group at two-thirds of the national figure sits 3 points under when the party is on 8%, and 9 under at 27%. So each group is tested on its gap in points and on its ratio to all voters, which holds steady for a group merely keeping pace, and a change counts only when both pass, in the same direction. Each test is a line through the monthly readings, tighter months counting for more. A group needs five monthly readings to be tested, and on seven or fewer the sentence hedges (“appears to”).Since the election. The sentence also asks whether a group has moved since the 2025 election, on the same two tests. It starts from the polls’ own figure for the group over the six weeks before polling day: the pollsters’ published tables then, pooled just as today’s figures are, and drawn on the charts as an open triangle. Both ends are the same polls, so a difference is movement. The Australian Election Study, a survey after the election of how people voted, is drawn beside it as a diamond. The margins can’t see pollsters defining or weighting a group differently, and a pollster that joins the series part-way, reading a group its own way, can move a monthly line by that alone. The test looks for steady change, so a rise and a fall within the period cancel out.

How settled the vote is. Some voters won’t name a party (undecided), some name one but might still change their mind (not firm), and the rest are firm. Economic sentiment1 entry

Five published confidence gauges of the same economy: the ANZ–Roy Morgan Consumer Confidence index, published weekly; the Westpac–Melbourne Institute Consumer Sentiment index, monthly; DemosAU’s national Consumer Sentiment Index, from November 2026; Roy Morgan’s Business Confidence index, monthly; and NAB’s Monthly Business Survey. The consumer lanes and the business pair sit in two views, switched by the tabs over the chart — the consumer lanes open first. The chart opens at the May 2025 election; the figures on file run further back — 2019 for the Roy Morgan series, 2022 for Westpac, and April 2025 for NAB.100 is neutral. The 100-neutral series are built from the balance of optimists and pessimists on each house’s questions, so a reading above 100 means optimists outnumber pessimists and one below 100 the reverse.NAB is drawn 100 points up. NAB reports a net balance — optimistic firms minus pessimistic ones — where 0 is neutral, so its dashed line is drawn 100 points up to share the neutral line with the others. The figure shown beside it and in its tooltips is NAB’s own printed number; the row also carries the survey’s conditions reading, which is not charted.DemosAU is drawn doubled. DemosAU’s national Consumer Sentiment Index puts 50 at the neutral mark, so its line is drawn doubled to share the neutral line with the others; read rows and tooltips carry DemosAU’s own figure.Smoothed, not averaged across sources. Each dot is a release’s own headline figure, linked to it; each line is the same readings smoothed with a recency-weighted kernel (half-life 14 days on the weekly consumer index, 60 days on the monthly series) so release-to-release noise reads as trend. Last-election flows.

The flow table a term’s implied 2PP is read through: the preferences counted at the election that opened the term. From 1987 it’s the only table anyone inside the term could have used (the earlier ones are reconstructions, below), so each past term sits on the same footing as today’s implied 2PP. The sitting term follows the rival Labor is doing worse against, as the headline does: the Coalition until June 2026, One Nation since, and the chart marks the change.How it’s worked out. A poll’s two-party figure for Labor is its Labor primary vote, plus each other group’s primary vote times that group’s flow to Labor in the term’s table, plus a small fixed correction: whatever the table leaves between the opening election’s own primary votes and its official result (Coalition preferences leaking to Labor in three-cornered seats, or the few seats a count didn’t cover), never more than 0.21 points. With it, each term starts exactly at the official result. A poll that doesn’t list a party separately reads it at the election’s own mix, and one whose shares don’t total 100 is rescaled first.Where the tables come from. From 2004, the AEC’s count of every ballot between Labor and the Coalition, in every seat. For 1996–2001, its official statistics, from the seats where the final two were Labor and the Coalition: 142 to 145 of the 148 to 150. Before 1996 the AEC published no flows by party. Every seat’s full distribution exists from 1984, and flows by party can be estimated from it (the preference flows explainer charts them), but the 1987–93 polls on file give one combined figure for the minor parties, so each of those terms uses the single minor-party flow its opening election’s official result implies, which reproduces that election exactly. The Morgan Gallup polls of 1943–84 published no two-party figure at all, so those terms are read through tables fitted to the two-party result of the election that opened each term. Each table solves one flow so that it reproduces that result: everyone else’s to 1954, the DLP’s to 1975, the Democrats’ from 1977. The rest are fixed: everyone else at 45%, and the DLP at 27% once its vote collapsed. Two are assumptions: the Democrats’ 50% in the 1975 table, since they had never stood, and the DLP’s 27% in 1955, the year of the Labor split, whose solved value (0.2%) misread 1957–58. With nothing solved, the 1955 table misses its own election by 1.4 points, so that term starts off its official result. Before 1984 those results are themselves estimates, by the Parliamentary Library and Malcolm Mackerras, so these tables are fitted to estimates, not counts. And since no one at the time had the flows, they reconstruct what a last-election reading would have given, rather than one anyone could have made.How well it works. Applied to the next election’s actual primary votes, each table since 1984 misses the counted 2PP by 0.4 points on average. The biggest misses were 2013 and 2022, when Labor did 1.0 points better than projected, and 2019, when it did 0.8 worse. The older tables miss by 0.4 on average, but before 1984 the official two-party results are themselves estimates, built partly on assumed preference flows, so those misses measure agreement with an estimate, not a count, and probably flatter the tables: the 1958–83 ones miss by only 0.2, and the 1955 table’s DLP flow was chosen with the 1958 result in view. Read their real error as at least the counted era’s. Either way the conversion is the smaller error: the final polls before 1984 missed the official two-party result by 2.2 points on average, almost all of it through their primary votes (Labor’s alone missed by 2.4).

Poll lean.

How far one poll sits from the other polls fielded around the same time, averaged as the headline is. It describes that poll, not the pollster: sampling luck alone can put a single poll off the pace. A pollster whose polls lean the same way again and again is showing its house effect.

Aggregate effect.

How much one poll moves the headline. The poll’s breakdown recalculates the weighted aggregate without it: “+0.1 for ALP” means today’s two-party figure is a tenth of a point higher because this poll is in the window. One line appears for each figure the poll feeds: the published two-party figure, the implied 2PP, and Labor v One Nation where the poll asked it. A poll older than 21 days moves nothing now, and says so, with what it was worth when published where that can still be worked out. House effects are left as measured, since they belong to the pollster, not the poll.

APC statement.

The methodology statement a pollster publishes for each poll under the Australian Polling Council’s Code of Conduct: fieldwork dates and method, the raw sample, the effective sample (what that sample is worth after weighting), the weighting used, and the question order. It is the pollster’s own publication; nothing is lodged with the council. A poll’s breakdown links it, and its effective sample sets the poll’s weight in the weighted aggregate. Newspoll, YouGov, Essential, DemosAU, RedBridge/Accent, and Fox & Hedgehog publish one. Where a pollster publishes none, the neff column shows a dash: the site doesn’t invent a figure the pollster never published.

MRP.

Multilevel regression and post-stratification: a model that estimates each seat separately instead of applying one national swing everywhere. Seat figures appear only where a pollster built such a model and published the results, and the MRP tag marks those polls.

Poll disagreement.

Whether the pollsters disagree more than chance explains. No two polls match even when opinion hasn’t moved, because each surveys a different sample. The panel compares how far the polls actually spread with how far their sample sizes alone would scatter them, and the ratio gives one of four verdicts:Tighter than chance (under 0.80×): closer together than independent samples can be, which suggests pollsters are adjusting towards each other.Within chance (0.80–1.20×): as close as sampling allows, which is what healthy polling looks like.A little beyond chance (1.20–1.60×): something real sits on top of chance, such as different weighting, different voters reached, or opinion moving at different speeds.Well beyond chance (1.60× or more): the gap can’t be chance. The pollsters aren’t measuring one number with different luck.

House lean.

Each pollster’s house effect traced month by month, so a change of method or ownership shows where it happened instead of being smeared across its history. The One Nation–Coalition split view is a pollster’s lean on One Nation’s primary vote less its lean on the Coalition’s: how it splits the right’s vote.
