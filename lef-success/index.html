How last-election preferences have performed

Kevin Bonham’s 2015 test, re-run on every election from 1987 to 2025

Last-election flows have missed the two-party vote by less than half a point on average

Most two-party figures, including this site’s, assume each party’s preferences will flow the way they did at the last election. Across 14 elections that assumption missed by 0.43 points on average (the final polls’ own primary votes err by more — 1.11 points on average even against the election’s actual flows), and by a third of a point or less eight times. The big misses were 2013 and 2022, when Labor did about a point better, and 2019, when the Coalition did 0.8 better. The misses don’t lean one way.

The test

In “Wonk Central: The Track Record Of Last-Election Preferences” (September 2015, with updates for 2016, 2019 and 2022), Bonham imagined a pollster that gets every party’s first-preference vote exactly right, then estimates the two-party vote using the previous election’s preference flows. The gap between that estimate and the actual two-party result measures only how much preference flows changed between elections. Nothing else can go wrong.

His rule for which parties the pollster tracks:

  • Labor and the Coalition, plus every minor party that won at least 4% of the vote at the previous election. Each is read at its own flow from that election.
  • Everyone else, including a party standing for the first time, is “Others”: read at the previous election’s combined flow for all the minor parties and independents that weren’t tracked.
  • One exception: the Democrats stay tracked for 1996, though they won only 3.75% in 1993.

So One Nation, new in 1998, was read as Others that year and tracked from 2001. The Greens were Others until 2004, the first election after they passed 4%.

Method, step by step

  1. Each election’s flowsFor every party, the share of its voters whose preferences ended with Labor rather than the Coalition. For Others, that share across all the untracked ballots combined.
  2. The table’s own correctionApplying an election’s flows to its own first preferences doesn’t quite reproduce its official two-party result. Seats where the final two weren’t Labor and the Coalition, and Liberal-National contests, leave a small gap (under half a point, and 0.15 or less since 2004). The gap is carried forward as a fixed adjustment, the same way the AEC-based tables on the rest of the site do.
  3. The next electionThe flows plus the adjustment are applied to the next election’s actual first preferences, giving the two-party vote last-election flows implied.
  4. The missThat figure minus the official two-party result, in points of Labor’s two-party vote. A negative miss means Labor did better than its last-election flows implied.

The average miss was 0.43 points across all 14 elections and 0.42 across the last twelve (1993–2025). Bonham reported 0.3 for his last twelve, 1983–2013; on the elections from 1987 to 2013 this re-run averages 0.39. Minor parties now take a third of the vote, so the same flow shifts would move the two-party figure further today. At 2025’s level of minor-party voting, the average miss would have been about 0.76 points.

No real pollster gets first preferences exactly. Put each house’s final poll’s own published primaries through the election’s own counted flows — the flows the pollster could not have known, so flow drift cannot contribute — and, scored the way the main site’s accuracy panel scores a final poll (one per house inside a fortnight of the election, house means equally weighted), the twelve elections since 1993 miss by an average of 1.11 points. Run through the last-election tables the same polls miss 1.10; the imaginary exact-primary pollster misses 0.44 on those twelve. The pollsters’ primaries, not the flow assumption, are where most of a real poll’s miss comes from.

Where the numbers come from

Every input is a published count, or an estimate built from one:

  • 2004–2025the AEC’s “two party preferred flow of preferences by state by party” for each election, from the AEC Tally Room downloads. These are counted flows for every party in every seat. First preferences and the official two-party vote come from the same file.
  • 1996–2001the AEC’s official election statistics packs: two-candidate preferred flows by party for every division. These are counted flows, but only for the seats where Labor and the Coalition were the final two (about nine seats in ten), because only those say where ballots ended between the two. First preferences come from the packs’ candidate table, and the two-party vote from their national summary.
  • 1984–1993the AEC published no flows by party for these elections, only each seat’s count, exclusion by exclusion (1984–90 transcribed by Adam Carr’s Psephos from the AEC’s printed volumes; 1993 from the statistics pack). A count records where an excluded candidate’s whole pile went, including votes it had picked up from earlier exclusions. This site’s preference estimator works out how much of each pile was the candidate’s own voters, and so each party’s own flow. It is the same model behind this site’s state-election flows. Labor’s and the Coalition’s first preferences and the two-party results are the AEC’s national figures. The split of the rest between the Democrats, Greens and others comes from the seat counts.
  • 1983 and earlierleft out. Counts then stopped as soon as a candidate passed half the vote, so most seats never reached a final two.

How much to trust it

  • The 1984–93 flows are estimatesThe model was checked on 1996–2001, where the AEC did count the Democrats by party. It put their flow to Labor 1.1 to 4.4 points too low (3.1 on average). Because the table’s own correction absorbs most of that, the effect on each miss is small: at most about 0.2 points (1990 and 1993).
  • 1996–2001 are counted, but over classic seats onlyThe tenth of seats left out had other final pairs. The table’s correction covers the gap that leaves.
  • One judgement call matters for 2025The UAP (4.1% in 2022) would be tracked, but it didn’t stand in 2025. Clive Palmer’s 2025 party was Trumpet of Patriots, a different registered party, so the strict rule reads its 1.9% as Others (54.7% to Labor). Reading it at the UAP’s 2022 flow instead (38.1%) moves the 2025 miss from +0.32 to 0.00.
  • The pollster is imaginaryReal polls get first preferences wrong too. This test isolates the preference assumption only. Priced in the Results section: the real final polls’ own primaries miss by 1.11 points even with each election’s actual flows in hand — two and a half times the imaginary pollster’s misses on the same elections.

Where it differs from Bonham’s figures

For 2013, 2016 and 2019 the re-run matches his published misses to within 0.05 points. Two elections differ:

  • 1990−0.68 here against his −1.1. Both say Labor beat its 1987 flows, which he puts down to its courting of Greens preferences. The gap is at least partly the estimated Democrat flow for 1987 (see above). His 1987 flows were his own estimates from the same seat counts, so the rest of the gap is two different estimates of the same thing.
  • 2022−0.87 here against his −0.99. Under the 4% rule, One Nation (3.1%) and the UAP (3.4%) count as Others in 2019. His 2022 update names their flows separately, which suggests he tracked them. Reading both at their own 2019 flows gives −0.95 here, within 0.04 of his figure.

The re-run doesn’t cover his elections before 1987. This site has flow tables for 1943–84, but their constants were fitted across whole decades of elections at once. Testing them on those same elections would flatter them.

This site’s own tables

The two-party figures on the rest of the site group voters differently: Greens, One Nation, and everyone else. That’s because those are the groups polls report. For 1987–93 they use one combined flow for all minor parties. Run through the same test, those tables average 0.42 points from 1990 to 2025, much the same as Bonham’s rule. Individual elections differ more: 1990 is −0.13 and 2025 is −0.39. So how voters are grouped matters at a given election, but the long-run record doesn’t depend on it.

Does asking voters do better?

Bonham argued that matching this record by asking respondents where their preferences would go would take a sample of about 15,000 minor-party voters per election. He didn’t show the working. A simple calculation from sampling error alone gives a much smaller number: about 400 minor-party voters at 2013’s level of minor-party voting, and about 950 at today’s. A 3,000-person poll now holds around 1,000. So sample size alone doesn’t rule respondent preferences out. The case against them is that respondents’ answers have missed how they actually voted. In 2004 the major pollsters expected 69–74% of minor-party preferences to reach Labor; 61% did.

Reproduce it

The code and its inputs are in the site’s public repository:

  • bonham-party-flows.py builds the flow tables by party (bonham-party-flows.json) from the cached AEC files in .build/aec-flow-src/, about three minutes for the model fit.
  • bonham-lef-track-record.mjs runs the test both ways (Bonham’s rule and the site’s groups), reruns the twelve elections from 1993 on the real final polls’ own primaries, and prints every figure on this page.
