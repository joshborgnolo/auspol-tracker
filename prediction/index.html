Will this government be re-elected?

A statistical model trained on the polling record of every completed federal term since 1987 reads the signature of Albanese’s second term, sixteen months in — as at 5 September 2026.

What this number is

The 78 per cent is not a poll of voters and it is not a betting price. It comes from a model that was shown the polling history of every completed federal parliamentary term since 1987 — thirteen terms, nine of which ended in the government’s re-election and four in its defeat — and taught to recognise the signature a term shows part-way through: how the prime minister is rated, whether the prime minister still leads as preferred PM, and how far the vote has faded from the last election result. Read against that record, the signature of Albanese’s second term at sixteen months is the signature of a government that goes on to survive.

The honest part of the answer is the width of the band. Thirteen terms and four defeats is a thin history, so no model of this kind can separate 78 from 85 per cent with confidence — the genuine range runs from an uncomfortable 40 to a comfortable 95. What the record can say clearly is which company this term keeps: at the same age, three of the four governments that eventually fell already looked markedly sicker than this one.

How the model reads this term

Five readings, all knowable from published polling, drive the call.

  • Net PM approval · −19 Voters disapprove of Albanese by a net margin of about nineteen points. On its own, that is a historically bad level — the four defeated governments all carried numbers like this, and a model that looks only at levels does in fact read this term worse than the typical loser.
  • Preferred-PM lead · +15 Albanese still leads the opposition leader by about fifteen points as preferred prime minister. Governments that lose usually lose the leadership question as well as the vote.
  • Primary vote · −6.5 on 2025 Labor’s primary is running about six and a half points under its election vote. That is a standard post-election fade — indistinguishable, in this record, from the fade most surviving governments wore.
  • Two-party preferred · −3.1 on 2025 The government remains narrowly in front, about three points off its election pace.
  • Age of the government · second term More than any single poll, age sorts the fates. No first-term government on this record has ever been voted out; the four that fell were in their second, third, fourth and fifth terms, at ages 2, 3, 4 and 5. This is the second consecutive Labor term — the same age at which the Gillard government was eventually ousted.

The blend is the point: bad approval levels, but an ordinary fade, a young government, and a lead on the two measures that most often persist. That is why the model lands well short of alarm — the terms that fell looked far sicker than this by the same month.

You can score 69 per cent on this table by simply always predicting “returned” — incumbents usually win. Tested honestly, with each term held out in turn and never seen by its judge, the model calls ten of thirteen terms correctly from month six onwards, and eleven by the final read on the eve of the campaign. The column that matters today is month 15, roughly where this term stands. The four eventual losers read 91, 91, 76 and 29 there. The nine winners read as low as 1 — and at worst 63, the Hawke government’s famous mid-term scare of 1989–90, from which it recovered to win the 1990 election. History is blur, with two tails that misbehave; the current term’s 24 sits inside the winning side of it.

Where this can go wrong

There are only thirteen terms. Four defeats is a tiny sample, and the final-read accuracy of 85 per cent itself carries about fourteen points of statistical noise either way. Treat every number on this page as a best guess with a range attached — which is why the range, not the median, is the most honest figure here.

It cannot see shocks. The model reads the shape of polling, not events. The 2010–13 minority government’s collapse has no antecedent in the record, and the model never calls it — at any horizon. The 2019–22 term flips depending on exactly when you look: the COVID rally peaks at precisely the fifteen-month yardstick. And 1990 is the one genuine comeback on record — a 71 per cent ouster-read at month 12 that still won.

Waiting buys less than you’d think. Accuracy is a flat 77 per cent from month six to month 24; only the final pre-election read climbs to 85. Governments that are structurally finished are visible early — three of the four losers were already long shots at six months. Everything else stays murky until the campaign.

Winning means seats, not the popular vote. Fates are coded by who formed government. Howard’s 1998 win on 49 per cent of the two-party vote counts as a win — the model is about survival, not vote share. And none of this is a forecast of events between now and the election: it is a reading of history’s signatures, and the election itself remains its own fact.

The construction. Each completed term contributes snapshots at ages 6, 12, 15, 18, 24 and 30 months plus a final read three months before its last day — ninety snapshots in all. Each snapshot’s features are trailing-three-month summaries knowable at that age: net PM approval, the preferred-PM lead, the primary-vote and two-party swings against the term’s own election result, the government’s incumbency age in consecutive terms, the fraction of the term elapsed (the current term is assumed to run a full span), and the interaction of primary swing with elapsed fraction — seven features in all. Missing values are median-imputed within the training fold; features are standardised within it; the fit is ridge logistic regression with λ = 1.

The validation. Leave-one-term-out: a term’s snapshots are scored only by a model trained on the other twelve. The baseline “always re-elected” scores 69 per cent. Snapshot-level AUC is 0.77 with Brier score 0.182. The interval comes from a 300-draw cluster bootstrap that resamples whole terms, refits, and re-scores the current term each draw; 18 per cent of draws cross the halfway line into “ousted”. The live call, verbatim:

p(ousted | profile at 16.2mo) = median 0.22 · 10–90% CI [0.05, 0.60] share of bootstrap draws calling ousted (p ≥ 0.5): 18% features: pmNet −18.9 · ppm +14.5 · primSw −6.5 · tppSw −3.1 · govAge 2 · ageFrac 0.44 (the table’s current-term row shows the in-sample profile read: 0.24)

The cross-check. A deliberately different construction — one ridge logistic per term on first-sixteen-month features — reads p(ousted) = 0.31: same verdict, well short of alarm. Its best-calibrated variant, adding leadership-spill and minority-government flags, holds the same 85 per cent leave-one-term-out accuracy with AUC 0.89 and Brier 0.134. On thirteen terms the estimator is not the constraint — a diagonal LDA ties the ridge and k-nearest-neighbours collapses — and no capacity beyond logistic earns its keep: adjacent accuracies are statistically indistinguishable (85 per cent carries a 95% Wilson interval of roughly [58%, 96%]).
